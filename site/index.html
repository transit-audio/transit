Hit record. It keeps up.

A free, open-source DAW for recording and playing live, on Windows, Mac and Linux. Latency settings are chosen from measurements on real audio interfaces, and the numbers are public.

Alpha. Current release: v0.7.2. Early software that changes every week: back up what you care about, and see the known issues.

Made for the take

  1. Track without plug-in latency.

    One click on Low Latency mode bypasses the slowest inserts on the tracks you are recording and monitoring. Turn it off and your mix is back as it was.

  2. A count-in that keeps your pickup.

    Notes you play during the count-in land in the take as pickups. One known limit: at the very start of a session, a note released before bar 1 is left out and the status line says so (known issues).

  3. Hardware, in the session.

    Hardware inserts measure their own round trip with a click train and delay compensation uses the result. External Instrument sends MIDI out and brings the audio back on the input you pick.

  4. A sampler built for kits and loops.

    Pads, Slices and Instrument views on one instrument: load a kit from a folder, cut a loop at its transients, and play it from your pads.

  5. Instruments worth opening.

    Built-in synths ported from Émilie Gillet’s MIT-licensed firmware and tested against the original code, in a design language made for reading at a glance.

  6. The mixer shows what is loaded.

    Mixer strips render on several cores, and the transport shows the load, buffer and driver at a glance. The measurements behind that default are public.

  7. Native ASIO, WASAPI and JACK.

    See every channel of your interface; name your own paths and route anything to them.

The instruments and effects ported from Émilie Gillet’s MIT-licensed firmware are rendered next to the original code and compared in tests. The differences we know of are written down. How the port is checked.
No resamplingat equal rates
When your interface runs at the session’s sample rate, the output goes straight through, with no sample-rate conversion. A test pins it. The test. When the rates differ, the converter uses plain linear interpolation, and improving it is tracked.
Real-time rulesfor the audio callback
A written rule: the audio callback never blocks, allocates unexpectedly or does I/O. The real-time crates are linted and tested on Windows, Linux and macOS. The principle and the workflow.
Every mergereviewed to break it
A second reviewer with fresh context tries to break each change before it merges. How the review works.

Why transit

Record and pick your best take

Record a part again and again. Every take stacks up in its own lane, so you choose the one that plays. Bounce in Place turns a track into audio when you are done.

Use the plug-ins you own

Plays CLAP and VST3 plug-ins. Open their editors in their own windows.

A sampler built for kits and loops

The Sampler holds a whole kit with three views: pads to play, slices to cut a loop at its hits, and key zones with velocity layers. Save a kit and load it again with one click.

Instruments and effects included

Comes with synths such as the Macro Oscillator, Resonator and Modal Voice, plus compressor, EQ, reverb and more.

Low delay, and it shows the number

On Windows, transit talks to ASIO and WASAPI directly, and the Audio Devices window shows the delay it measures.

Also in the box

  • A mixer with buses, sends and sidechains.
  • Automation for volume, pan and plug-in knobs.
  • A piano roll with scale and quantize tools.
  • MIDI controllers that learn: move a knob, and it maps to what you picked.
  • Plays hardware synths and records them.
  • Export to WAV, AIFF and FLAC, or Opus for sharing.
  • Opens and exports DAWproject files, to move songs to and from other DAWs.
  • Tracks as tall or as short as you like, with the grid drawn over the clips if you want it.
  • Low Latency mode: skips the slowest effects on the tracks you play, so you hear yourself sooner.
  • Start and stop from your MIDI gear's transport buttons.
  • Runs on Linux too.

How we decide

For the curious: how changes get into transit.

Measure first

We change a setting only after measuring it on real hardware, and we publish the numbers.

Mistakes become checks

When a bug slips through, we write what happened and add a check that catches it.

Decisions are written down

Every design change gets a short written decision with its evidence, and every change is reviewed by someone trying to break it.

Known problems are public

What we know is broken is in the known issues list.
